01

What an Option Is

An option is a contract that gives its holder the right but not the obligation to trade an underlying asset $S$ at a fixed price $K$, on or before a fixed date $T$. The holder pays the writer a premium up front in exchange for that right.

"An option is the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying at a prescribed price at a prescribed time in the future."

— Wilmott, Ch. 2

That single asymmetry — right, not obligation — is what separates an option from a forward (where you must transact) and is the entire reason an option has a non-trivial value.

Call option

The right to buy $S$ at $K$.

Worth something when $S$ rises above $K$. Worthless if $S < K$ at expiry: don't exercise, walk away.

$$\text{payoff at }T = \max(S_T - K, 0).$$

Put option

The right to sell $S$ at $K$.

Worth something when $S$ falls below $K$. Worthless if $S > K$ at expiry.

$$\text{payoff at }T = \max(K - S_T, 0).$$

The four cardinal positions

Every option strategy in this book is a sum of these four legs:

PositionYou pay/receivePayoff at $T$View
long callpay $C$$+(S_T-K)^+$bullish, limited downside
short callreceive $C$$-(S_T-K)^+$bearish/neutral, unlimited risk
long putpay $P$$+(K-S_T)^+$bearish, limited downside
short putreceive $P$$-(K-S_T)^+$bullish/neutral, capped upside
Asymmetry, in one line

The buyer chooses whether to exercise; the writer must comply. The writer is paid the premium to take on that obligation.

02

Definitions: Strike, Expiry, Premium, Moneyness

The contract is fully specified by a handful of parameters; the rest of the book is about pricing them.

Contract parameters

  • Strike $K$ — the price at which the holder may transact.
  • Expiry $T$ — the last date on which the right exists.
  • Premium $C$ or $P$ — what the buyer pays today.
  • Underlying $S$ — the asset the option is written on.

Exercise style

  • European — exercise only at $T$.
  • American — exercise at any time $t \le T$.
  • Bermudan — exercise on a pre-specified set of dates.

The book mostly prices Europeans; American early-exercise is treated by free-boundary problems (Deck 03 and onward).

Moneyness

StateCallPut
in the money (ITM)$S > K$$S < K$
at the money (ATM)$S = K$$S = K$
out of the money (OTM)$S < K$$S > K$

Intrinsic and time value

The premium decomposes into

$$C = \underbrace{\max(S-K,0)}_{\text{intrinsic}} + \underbrace{C - \max(S-K,0)}_{\text{time value}}.$$

The intrinsic value is what the option would pay if exercised immediately. The time value is everything else — the option holder's chance that the underlying moves further in their favour before expiry. For a European option, time value $\ge 0$; it decays to zero as $t \to T$.

Why an OTM option still has value

Even if $S < K$ today and the call is worthless if exercised now, there is still some probability the spot moves above $K$ before $T$. That residual probability — weighted by the discounted payoff — is the option's time value.

03

Payoff Diagrams

Payoff diagrams plot the value of the position at expiry as a function of $S_T$. They ignore the premium — that comes in when we plot profit.

Long call

$$V_T = \max(S_T - K, 0).$$

Flat at zero below $K$, slope $+1$ above. Unlimited upside; downside capped at the premium $C$.

Short call

$$V_T = -\max(S_T - K, 0).$$

Mirror image. Best case: keep the premium and walk away. Worst case: unlimited loss.

Long put

$$V_T = \max(K - S_T, 0).$$

Slope $-1$ below $K$, flat above. Best when the underlying collapses; bounded loss = premium.

Short put

$$V_T = -\max(K - S_T, 0).$$

Maximum gain = premium. Loss bounded only by $S_T \ge 0$; equivalent to being long the stock plus a fixed liability.

Mirror principle

Buyer's payoff + Writer's payoff = $0$ at every $S_T$. The option is a zero-sum contract; the premium is the price at which both sides agree the bet is fair.

04

Writing Options, Margin, Market Conventions

Buying an option is simple: pay the premium up front, the most you can lose is the premium. Writing an option is much more dangerous — you receive a small premium and take on a potentially large liability.

Margin

To stop writers walking away, the exchange demands margin — collateral posted with the clearing house. The margin is marked-to-market daily; if it falls below maintenance level the writer must top it up or the position is closed out.

Margin requirements for naked short calls are particularly punishing because the loss is unbounded.

Contract size and conventions

One listed equity option in the US covers 100 shares. Quoted premiums are per share. FX options are quoted as a percentage of notional; index options are cash-settled because you can't deliver an index.

Expiry is almost always a Friday; American style is the norm for listed equity options, European for index options.

The four ways to take a position

ActionCash todayWhat you owe
buy a call$-C$nothing (you have a right)
sell a call$+C$$\max(S_T-K,0)$, plus margin
buy a put$-P$nothing
sell a put$+P$$\max(K-S_T,0)$, plus margin
Wilmott's warning

"Writing options is not an activity to undertake casually." Long positions risk only the premium; short positions risk the entire payoff, which is why margin exists.

05

Put–Call Parity

Calls and puts on the same underlying, with the same strike and expiry, are not independent — they are linked by a no-arbitrage identity. Consider two portfolios at $t = 0$:

Portfolio A

  • long one call (premium $C$)
  • short one put (receive $P$)

Payoff at $T$:

$$\max(S_T-K,0) - \max(K-S_T,0) = S_T - K.$$

Portfolio B

  • long one share (cost $S_0$)
  • short $K e^{-rT}$ in cash (borrow it)

Payoff at $T$:

$$S_T - K.$$

Both portfolios deliver the same payoff at every $S_T$. By no-arbitrage they must cost the same today:

$$\boxed{\;C - P = S - K e^{-rT}.\;}$$

This is put–call parity. It is model-free — no stochastic process, no volatility, no Black–Scholes — it follows from no-arbitrage alone, exactly like the forward price in Deck 01.

Synthetic stock

Rearrange:

$$S = C - P + K e^{-rT}.$$

A long call plus a short put plus a deposit of $K e^{-rT}$ is a synthetic share. You can build any one leg from the others; that is why a market in calls fully determines the market in puts.

Practical use

Traders watch the parity residual $C - P - S + K e^{-rT}$ in real time. A persistent non-zero value signals either a quoting error or a hidden cost (borrow fee, dividend assumption, hard-to-borrow stock).

06

Binaries / Digital Options

A binary (or digital) option pays a fixed amount if the underlying ends in a specified region at expiry, and zero otherwise.

Cash-or-nothing call

Pays $1$ if $S_T > K$, else $0$.

$$V_T = \mathbf{1}_{\{S_T > K\}}.$$

A pure bet on direction; no exposure to the magnitude of the move.

Asset-or-nothing call

Pays $S_T$ if $S_T > K$, else $0$.

$$V_T = S_T\, \mathbf{1}_{\{S_T > K\}}.$$

The ordinary call is the difference: cash-or-nothing pays $K$ on the same event, so $C_{\text{vanilla}} = V_{\text{asset}} - K\, V_{\text{cash}}$.

Binaries are useful as building blocks. Any payoff $f(S_T)$ that is piecewise linear in $S_T$ can be written as a portfolio of digital and vanilla options, which is how exotic books are static-hedged in practice.

Why traders hate digitals near expiry

The payoff is a step function. As $t \to T$ and $S$ approaches $K$, the binary's delta and gamma blow up — tiny moves in the spot flip the payoff from $0$ to $1$. The retail-friendly "binary option" of online broker fame is the same instrument, and the same risk-management nightmare.

07

Vertical Spreads — Bulls and Bears

A vertical spread is two options of the same type and expiry, different strikes. The first family of strategies that bound both the upside and the downside.

Bull call spread

Long a call at $K_1$, short a call at $K_2 > K_1$.

$$V_T = \max(S_T - K_1, 0) - \max(S_T - K_2, 0).$$

Payoff: $0$ below $K_1$, slope $+1$ between $K_1$ and $K_2$, capped at $K_2 - K_1$ above.

Cheaper than an outright call; you've sold the tail.

Bear put spread

Long a put at $K_2$, short a put at $K_1 < K_2$.

$$V_T = \max(K_2 - S_T, 0) - \max(K_1 - S_T, 0).$$

Payoff: capped at $K_2 - K_1$ below $K_1$, slope $-1$ between $K_1$ and $K_2$, $0$ above.

Bearish view with bounded loss.

Why traders use spreads

  • Cheaper premium. Selling a wing pays for some of the long leg.
  • Bounded P&L. Both maximum profit and maximum loss are known at trade entry.
  • Lower vega and gamma than an outright option — the two legs cancel partially.
Naming convention

"Bull" = the spread profits if the market rises; "bear" = profits if the market falls. The names are loose — you can build a bull spread out of either calls or puts — what matters is the slope of $V_T(S_T)$.

08

Straddles, Strangles, Risk Reversals

These are volatility strategies: trades whose P&L depends on how much $S$ moves, not where it goes.

Straddle

Long one call and one put, same strike $K$ (usually ATM).

$$V_T = |S_T - K|.$$

V-shape with minimum at $K$. Profitable if $S$ moves far enough in either direction to overcome the combined premium.

Strangle

Long a call at $K_2$ and a put at $K_1 < K_2$.

Cheaper than a straddle (both legs OTM) but needs a bigger move to pay off. Flat between the strikes, V-shaped outside.

Risk reversal

Long a call at $K_2$, short a put at $K_1 < K_2$ (or vice versa).

Used in FX. A synthetic forward with a "dead zone" between the strikes — cheap or even zero-cost depending on the skew.

09

Butterflies, Condors, Calendars and OTC Cousins

One more turn of the screw: three options instead of two, four instead of three, different expiries instead of different strikes.

Butterfly

Long at $K_1$ and $K_3$, short two at $K_2 = (K_1+K_3)/2$.

Tent-shaped payoff peaking at $K_2$. Profits if $S_T$ lands near $K_2$. A bounded short-vol play.

Condor

Long at $K_1, K_4$; short at $K_2, K_3$ with $K_1 < K_2 < K_3 < K_4$.

Flat-topped trapezoid. Like a butterfly with a wider sweet spot.

Calendar spread

Same strike, different expiries: short a near-dated option, long a far-dated one (or vice versa).

Trades the term structure of volatility and the differential theta decay.

OTC cousins (brief)

  • Warrants. Long-dated call options issued by a company on its own stock. Exercising creates new shares (dilutes).
  • Convertible bonds. Corporate bonds with an embedded call on the issuer's equity — bond + option in a single instrument.
  • LEAPS. Exchange-listed options with expiries out to several years; otherwise identical to ordinary listed options.
  • OTC options. Negotiated bilaterally with a bank; no exchange, no margin, but full counterparty risk. Strikes, expiries and payoffs are bespoke.
Building block view

Every payoff on this slide is a finite sum of vanilla calls and puts — the same four cardinal positions of slide 01. The whole catalogue of strategies is just linear algebra over those four basis vectors.
